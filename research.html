My research

From my undergraduate studies at the University of Milan to my PhD at the European Southern Observatory, I have been investigating how planets form by analyzing radio observations of protoplanetary disks — the birthplaces of planets — obtained with the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) and the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA).

Ph.D.

European Southern Observatory (ESO)

V-SHARDS III: Can planetesimals form in GM Aur's rings?

Viscardi et al., in prep.

Dust grains in protoplanetary disks collide at high relative speeds. As a result, they tend to bounce or break apart rather than stick, and struggle to grow beyond millimeter sizes. This raises a question: how do planetesimals, the kilometer-sized rocky bodies that are the building blocks of planets, form?

The leading explanation is a process called the streaming instability (SI). In regions where dust accumulates, such as dust rings, the aerodynamic interaction between dust and gas can trigger a positive feedback loop: dust clumps slow the gas, which lets more dust drift into the clumps. When the clumps become massive enough, they collapse under their own gravity and form planetesimals. Whether the SI can operate depends on three parameters: the dust-to-gas ratio, the Stokes number (the dust–gas coupling), and the level of turbulence.

After measuring the dust properties of GM Aur's rings in V-SHARDS II, I quantified their gas content and turbulence through a kinematic analysis of the 12CO and 13CO emission. Comparing the results with SI simulations shows that the planet-formation potential of GM Aur depends on its dust composition. For dust mixtures including refractory organics as carbonaceous materials, both rings lie well within the strong clumping regime, even for high turbulence (10−3), while for mixtures with amorphous carbons planetesimal formation is suppressed. These findings highlight the critical role of dust composition in assessing the planet-forming potential of disk rings.

V-SHARDS II: Massive reservoirs of solids in the rings of GM Aur

Viscardi et al., submitted

As part of the VLA Study at High Angular Resolution of Disk Substructures (V-SHARDS) Large Proposal (PI: Macías), high-quality 9 mm VLA observations have been obtainted for the very famous disk GM Aur. Since emission at different wavelengths probes different aspects of the dust grain population, combining these data with ALMA observations at 1.1 mm and 2.1 mm allows us to characterize the dust population and measure the radial distribution of dust temperature, dust surface density, and maximum grain size.

The two bright rings of GM Aur, at 40 au and 84 au, host massive reservoirs of solids (Mdust ≳ 70 M⊕ and ≳ 40 M⊕, respectively), enough to form several rocky planets and cores of giant planets, and contain centimeter-sized dust agglomerates. The exact masses and grain sizes depend on the assumed dust composition, a critical but highly uncertain parameter, so I explored a grid of porosities and two limiting carbon compositions: refractory organics or amorphous carbons.

A guide to multi-wavelength dust analysis of protoplanetary disks

Viscardi et al. 2025, A&A, 695, A147

ALMA and VLA continuum observations probe the thermal emission from dust grains in protoplanetary disks. Since different wavelengths are sensitive to different parts of the dust grain population, simultaneously modeling observations across multiple wavelengths allows us to reconstruct the radial distribution of dust and constrain its properties, including dust temperature, dust mass, and grain size. However, this approach requires extensive observations and can be affected by uncertainties that are difficult to anticipate.

I benchmarked this widely used technique on synthetic observations of disks to determine which wavelengths, and how many of them, are needed, and what spatial resolution (relative to the width of the substructures) is required to recover their physical properties. A key result is that long-wavelength VLA observations are essential to probe the dense interiors of substructures and derive accurate dust masses. The work also identifies the most economical way to perform these analyses, providing a guide for future observations.

Tracing disk evolution through the extent of CO isotopologue emission

Master's thesis

Protostars grow by accreting material from the disks that surround them. For this material to spiral inward toward the star, it must lose angular momentum. Two key mechanisms drive this process: turbulent viscosity, which makes disks spread outward over time, and MHD winds, which extract angular momentum vertically. Measuring disk radii across different evolutionary stages can therefore help distinguish between the two scenarios.

I measured the radial extents of 12CO, 13CO, and C18O emission in a sample of compact, faint disks, and compared them with bright, extended disks from the MAPS Large Program and with disk evolution models. The comparison could not conclusively identify the dominant mechanism, but it revealed a robust trend: the ratio of the 13CO to 12CO emitting radii stays remarkably constant at ∼0.7, regardless of disk mass, morphology, or size. This suggests that 13CO, often assumed to be optically thin, may remain optically thick in many disks, with important implications for gas mass estimates.

University of Milan

Tackling the "mass budget" problem through gas kinematics

Lodato et al. 2023, MNRAS, 518, 4481: Dynamical mass measurements of two protoplanetary discs

The disk mass sets the budget available to form planets, but traditional tracers such as single-wavelength millimeter dust continuum and CO isotopologue emission systematically underestimate it, because of uncertainties in dust opacity and molecular abundances. With researchers at the University of Milan and ESO, I helped test a novel technique based on gas kinematics: in sufficiently massive disks (disk-to-star mass ratio >5%), the disk's self-gravity makes the gas deviate from pure Keplerian rotation, allowing an estimate of the total disk mass independent of uncertain conversion factors. We published refined mass estimates for two disks, IM Lup and GM Aur.
